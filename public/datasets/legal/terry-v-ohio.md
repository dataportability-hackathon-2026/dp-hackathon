# Terry v. Ohio

**Citation:** 392 U.S. 1 (1968)
**Year:** 1968
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Criminal Procedure

## Facts
Cleveland police detective Martin McFadden observed John Terry and two other men repeatedly walking past a store window, peering inside, and conferring with each other. Based on his 39 years of experience, McFadden suspected the men were casing the store for a robbery. He approached the men, identified himself as a police officer, and patted down their outer clothing. He found revolvers on Terry and one of the other men.

## Issue
Whether a police officer may stop and frisk a person without probable cause for arrest if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person is involved in criminal activity and may be armed and dangerous.

## Holding
The Court held 8-1 that a police officer may stop a person for brief questioning and conduct a limited pat-down search for weapons if the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot and that the person may be armed and dangerous. This standard is lower than probable cause. The frisk must be limited to a pat-down of outer clothing for weapons and cannot extend to a full search.

## Significance
Terry v. Ohio created the "stop and frisk" doctrine, establishing that brief investigatory stops based on reasonable suspicion do not violate the Fourth Amendment. The decision balanced law enforcement needs against individual privacy rights by creating a tier of police-citizen encounters below the threshold of a full arrest. The ruling has been both praised for enabling effective policing and criticized for enabling racial profiling.

## Key Quotes
> "Where a police officer observes unusual conduct which leads him reasonably to conclude in light of his experience that criminal activity may be afoot, he is entitled for the protection of himself and others in the area to conduct a carefully limited search of the outer clothing."
