# Riley v. California

**Citation:** 573 U.S. 373 (2014)
**Year:** 2014
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Data Privacy

## Facts
David Riley was stopped for a traffic violation and subsequently arrested on weapons charges. During the arrest, officers searched his smartphone without a warrant and found photos, videos, and text messages linking him to gang activity and a prior shooting. The evidence from the phone was used to charge Riley with additional offenses. A second consolidated case, United States v. Wurie, involved a similar warrantless cell phone search.

## Issue
Whether the police may, without a warrant, search digital information on a cell phone seized from an individual who has been arrested.

## Holding
The Court held unanimously that police generally may not search a cell phone without a warrant during an arrest. Chief Justice Roberts distinguished cell phones from the physical items traditionally subject to search incident to arrest, noting that cell phones hold vast quantities of personal data that implicate far greater privacy interests. Neither the interest in officer safety nor the interest in preventing destruction of evidence justified dispensing with the warrant requirement.

## Significance
Riley established that the search-incident-to-arrest exception does not apply to cell phone data due to the immense storage capacity and deeply personal nature of information contained on modern smartphones. The decision acknowledged that cell phones are now such a pervasive part of daily life that the proverbial visitor from Mars might conclude they were an important feature of human anatomy.

## Key Quotes
> "Our answer to the question of what police must do before searching a cell phone seized incident to an arrest is accordingly simple — get a warrant."
