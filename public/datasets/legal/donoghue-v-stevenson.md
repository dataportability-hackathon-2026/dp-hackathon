# Donoghue v. Stevenson

**Citation:** [1932] AC 562
**Year:** 1932
**Court:** House of Lords (United Kingdom)
**Topic Area:** Torts

## Facts
May Donoghue went to a cafe in Paisley, Scotland, where a friend bought her a bottle of ginger beer manufactured by David Stevenson. After drinking most of the beer, Donoghue discovered the decomposed remains of a snail in the bottle. She suffered gastroenteritis and nervous shock as a result. Because she had not purchased the drink herself, she had no contractual claim against the shopkeeper and instead sued the manufacturer directly in negligence.

## Issue
Whether a manufacturer of products owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer when there is no contractual relationship between them, and the products are sold in a form that prevents intermediate examination.

## Holding
The House of Lords held 3-2 that manufacturers owe a duty of care to consumers who are the ultimate users of their products. Lord Atkin articulated the "neighbour principle," stating that a person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which they can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure their neighbour. A neighbour is any person so closely and directly affected by one's act that they ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.

## Significance
Donoghue v. Stevenson is considered the foundational case for the modern law of negligence in the common law world. Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" established the general duty of care concept that forms the basis of negligence claims. The decision influenced tort law across the Commonwealth and is frequently cited alongside MacPherson v. Buick as establishing manufacturer liability to end consumers.

## Key Quotes
> "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."
