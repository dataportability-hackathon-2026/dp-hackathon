# United States v. Jones

**Citation:** 565 U.S. 400 (2012)
**Year:** 2012
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Data Privacy

## Facts
Law enforcement officers suspected Antoine Jones of drug trafficking. Without a valid warrant, agents installed a GPS tracking device on the undercarriage of his wife's vehicle and monitored its movements for 28 days. The tracking generated over 2,000 pages of location data. Jones was convicted, but the D.C. Circuit reversed, holding that the warrantless use of the GPS device violated the Fourth Amendment.

## Issue
Whether the government's installation of a GPS tracking device on a suspect's vehicle and its use to monitor the vehicle's movements constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment.

## Holding
The Court held unanimously that the government's physical installation of a GPS device on Jones's vehicle and subsequent use of that device to track his movements constituted a search under the Fourth Amendment. Justice Scalia's majority opinion relied on the trespassory test, finding that the government physically occupied private property for the purpose of obtaining information. Justice Alito's concurrence argued the case should be decided under the Katz reasonable-expectation-of-privacy framework.

## Significance
Jones revived the property-based approach to Fourth Amendment analysis alongside the Katz privacy framework. The concurring opinions by Justices Alito and Sotomayor foreshadowed the Court's later holding in Carpenter v. United States, discussing how long-term surveillance can reveal intimate details of a person's life. The case highlighted growing concerns about government use of technology to conduct pervasive monitoring.

## Key Quotes
> "We hold that the Government's installation of a GPS device on a target's vehicle, and its use of that device to monitor the vehicle's movements, constitutes a 'search.'"
