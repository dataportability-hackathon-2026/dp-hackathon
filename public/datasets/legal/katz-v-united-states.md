# Katz v. United States

**Citation:** 389 U.S. 347 (1967)
**Year:** 1967
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Data Privacy

## Facts
Charles Katz was convicted of transmitting wagering information by telephone across state lines. The FBI had attached an electronic listening device to the outside of a public telephone booth that Katz regularly used, recording his conversations without a warrant. The government argued that no Fourth Amendment search occurred because there was no physical intrusion into the booth.

## Issue
Whether the Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches and seizures require a physical trespass, or whether they extend to conversations where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy.

## Holding
The Court held 7-1 that the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places, and that what a person seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public, may be constitutionally protected. The wiretapping of the phone booth constituted a search and seizure under the Fourth Amendment, and the government needed a warrant. Justice Harlan's concurrence established the influential two-part "reasonable expectation of privacy" test.

## Significance
Katz fundamentally shifted Fourth Amendment analysis from a property-based approach to a privacy-based approach. Justice Harlan's concurrence established the test used to this day: a person must exhibit a subjective expectation of privacy, and that expectation must be one that society recognizes as reasonable. This framework has shaped decades of privacy law, including cases involving digital surveillance.

## Key Quotes
> "The Fourth Amendment protects people, not places. What a person knowingly exposes to the public, even in his own home or office, is not a subject of Fourth Amendment protection. But what he seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public, may be constitutionally protected."
