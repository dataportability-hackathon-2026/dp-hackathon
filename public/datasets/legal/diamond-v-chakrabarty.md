# Diamond v. Chakrabarty

**Citation:** 447 U.S. 303 (1980)
**Year:** 1980
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Intellectual Property / Technology

## Facts
Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty, a microbiologist, filed a patent application for a genetically engineered bacterium capable of breaking down crude oil, which could be used in treating oil spills. The patent examiner allowed claims for the method of producing the bacterium but rejected claims for the bacterium itself, arguing that living organisms were not patentable subject matter. The Board of Patent Appeals affirmed the rejection.

## Issue
Whether a live, human-made micro-organism constitutes patentable subject matter under 35 U.S.C. Section 101.

## Holding
The Court held 5-4 that a live, human-made micro-organism is patentable subject matter. Chief Justice Burger's opinion found that the relevant distinction is not between living and inanimate things, but between products of nature and human-made inventions. Chakrabarty's bacterium was not a naturally occurring organism but a product of human ingenuity with markedly different characteristics from anything found in nature. Congress intended patent law to cover "anything under the sun that is made by man."

## Significance
Diamond v. Chakrabarty opened the door to biotechnology patents and is considered a foundational case for the biotech industry. The decision enabled the patenting of genetically modified organisms, contributing to billions of dollars in biotechnology investment and research. It established that patent law is broad enough to encompass new technologies not envisioned by the drafters of the patent statute.

## Key Quotes
> "The fact that micro-organisms are alive is without legal significance for purposes of the patent law. His discovery is not nature's handiwork, but his own."
