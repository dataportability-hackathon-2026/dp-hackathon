# Gideon v. Wainwright

**Citation:** 372 U.S. 335 (1963)
**Year:** 1963
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Criminal Procedure

## Facts
Clarence Earl Gideon was charged with breaking and entering a poolroom in Panama City, Florida, a felony under state law. Too poor to afford an attorney, Gideon requested that the court appoint one for him. The trial judge denied the request, as Florida law only required appointment of counsel in capital cases. Gideon represented himself, was convicted, and sentenced to five years in prison. From his cell, he filed a handwritten petition to the Supreme Court.

## Issue
Whether the Sixth Amendment's right to counsel in criminal cases is incorporated through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause and applies to state court proceedings.

## Holding
The Court held unanimously that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of counsel is a fundamental right essential to a fair trial, and it is made obligatory upon the states by the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court overruled Betts v. Brady (1942), which had held that appointed counsel was required only in special circumstances. Justice Black wrote that lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries.

## Significance
Gideon established the right to appointed counsel for all felony defendants who cannot afford an attorney, fundamentally transforming the criminal justice system. The decision led to the creation of public defender systems across the country and is considered one of the most important criminal procedure decisions in American history. Gideon's case was later retried with appointed counsel, and he was acquitted.

## Key Quotes
> "Lawyers in criminal courts are necessities, not luxuries. The right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours."
