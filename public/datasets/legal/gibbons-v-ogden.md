# Gibbons v. Ogden

**Citation:** 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824)
**Year:** 1824
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Constitutional Law

## Facts
Aaron Ogden held a license from the state of New York granting him a monopoly to operate steamboats between New York and New Jersey. Thomas Gibbons operated competing steamboats under a federal coasting license. Ogden sued to enforce his monopoly, and the New York courts ruled in his favor. Gibbons appealed to the Supreme Court.

## Issue
Whether Congress has the power under the Commerce Clause to regulate navigation between states, and whether a federal license supersedes a state-granted monopoly.

## Holding
Chief Justice Marshall held that the Commerce Clause grants Congress broad power to regulate interstate commerce, including navigation. The Court defined commerce broadly to include all commercial intercourse between states, not merely the buying and selling of goods. The federal coasting license held by Gibbons superseded the New York monopoly grant, as federal law is supreme under the Supremacy Clause.

## Significance
Gibbons v. Ogden was the first major Commerce Clause case and established a broad interpretation of congressional power over interstate commerce. The decision opened waterways to competition, promoting economic growth, and laid the groundwork for the expansive federal regulatory power that would develop over the next two centuries.

## Key Quotes
> "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more: it is intercourse. It describes the commercial intercourse between nations, and parts of nations, in all its branches."
