# Batson v. Kentucky

**Citation:** 476 U.S. 79 (1986)
**Year:** 1986
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Criminal Procedure

## Facts
James Kirkland Batson, a Black man, was tried for second-degree burglary and receipt of stolen goods in Kentucky. During jury selection, the prosecutor used peremptory challenges to remove all four Black persons from the venire, resulting in an all-white jury. Batson's attorney moved to discharge the jury on the ground that the removal of the Black jurors violated Batson's Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment rights. The trial court denied the motion, and Batson was convicted.

## Issue
Whether a prosecutor's use of peremptory challenges to exclude jurors on the basis of race violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding
The Court held 7-2 that the Equal Protection Clause forbids the prosecution from challenging potential jurors solely on account of their race. The Court established a three-step framework: first, the defendant must make a prima facie case of racial discrimination; second, the prosecution must offer a race-neutral explanation for the challenge; third, the trial court must determine whether the defendant has proven purposeful discrimination.

## Significance
Batson fundamentally changed jury selection procedures by creating a mechanism to challenge racially motivated peremptory strikes. The decision has been extended to apply to defense attorneys, civil cases, and discrimination based on gender. The three-step Batson framework remains the standard procedure for challenging allegedly discriminatory peremptory challenges in courts across the country.

## Key Quotes
> "The harm from discriminatory jury selection extends beyond that inflicted on the defendant and the excluded juror to touch the entire community. Selection procedures that purposefully exclude black persons from juries undermine public confidence in the fairness of our system of justice."
