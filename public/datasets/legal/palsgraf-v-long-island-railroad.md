# Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co.

**Citation:** 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928)
**Year:** 1928
**Court:** New York Court of Appeals
**Topic Area:** Torts

## Facts
A man carrying a package rushed to board a moving train at a Long Island Rail Road station. Two railroad employees helped him aboard, but the package — which contained fireworks — was dislodged and fell onto the tracks. The fireworks exploded, causing a scale at the other end of the platform to fall and injure Helen Palsgraf, who was standing nearby. Palsgraf sued the railroad for negligence.

## Issue
Whether a defendant owes a duty of care to a plaintiff who is injured as an unforeseeable consequence of the defendant's negligent act toward another person.

## Holding
Chief Judge Cardozo, writing for the majority, held that the railroad was not liable because it owed no duty to Palsgraf. The risk of harm to a person standing at the far end of the platform from a package of unknown contents was not foreseeable. Negligence requires a duty to the particular plaintiff, and duty is defined by the foreseeability of harm. Judge Andrews dissented, arguing that duty runs to all persons who are injured as a proximate result of negligent conduct.

## Significance
Palsgraf is one of the most famous and frequently studied tort cases in American law schools. It established the principle that liability in negligence is limited to foreseeable plaintiffs. The majority and dissenting opinions represent two competing frameworks for analyzing proximate cause and duty that continue to influence tort law today.

## Key Quotes
> "The risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed, and risk imports relation; it is risk to another or to others within the range of apprehension."
