# MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.

**Citation:** 217 N.Y. 382, 111 N.E. 1050 (1916)
**Year:** 1916
**Court:** New York Court of Appeals
**Topic Area:** Torts

## Facts
Donald MacPherson purchased a Buick automobile from a dealer. While driving, one of the car's wheels collapsed because it was made of defective wood. MacPherson was injured and sued Buick Motor Company directly, even though he had purchased the car from a dealer rather than from Buick. Buick argued it had no duty to MacPherson because there was no direct contractual relationship (privity of contract) between them. Buick had purchased the wheel from a subcontractor.

## Issue
Whether a manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of its product when there is no direct contractual relationship between them.

## Holding
Judge Cardozo held that a manufacturer of a product that is reasonably certain to be dangerous if negligently made owes a duty of care not only to the immediate purchaser but to all persons who might foreseeably use the product. The manufacturer's duty does not depend on privity of contract. Buick was liable because it could have discovered the defect through reasonable inspection and the automobile was a dangerous instrumentality when defective.

## Significance
MacPherson effectively eliminated the privity requirement in negligence cases involving manufactured products. The decision is considered the foundation of modern product liability law, extending manufacturer liability to all foreseeable users regardless of contractual relationships. Judge Cardozo's opinion influenced courts nationwide and paved the way for strict product liability doctrines developed later in the twentieth century.

## Key Quotes
> "If the nature of a thing is such that it is reasonably certain to place life and limb in peril when negligently made, it is then a thing of danger."
