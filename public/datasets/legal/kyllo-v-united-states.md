# Kyllo v. United States

**Citation:** 533 U.S. 27 (2001)
**Year:** 2001
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Data Privacy

## Facts
Federal agents suspected Danny Kyllo of growing marijuana in his home using high-intensity lamps. Without a warrant, agents used a thermal imaging device from across the street to scan his triplex home. The scan showed that the roof and a side wall were relatively hot compared to neighboring units, suggesting the use of halide lights. Based on the thermal imaging results, tips, and utility bills, agents obtained a warrant and found a marijuana growing operation.

## Issue
Whether the use of a thermal imaging device aimed at a private home from a public vantage point to detect relative amounts of heat constitutes a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment.

## Holding
The Court held 5-4 that using sense-enhancing technology to obtain information about the interior of a home that could not otherwise have been obtained without physical intrusion constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment, at least where the technology is not in general public use. The warrantless use of the thermal imager was therefore an unreasonable search.

## Significance
Kyllo established an important precedent for technology-assisted surveillance, holding that the Fourth Amendment draws a firm line at the entrance to the home. The decision anticipated future technologies that could erode privacy protections and established that the use of technology not in general public use to explore details of a home requires a warrant. The ruling has been cited in subsequent digital privacy cases.

## Key Quotes
> "In the home, our cases show, all details are intimate details, because the entire area is held safe from prying government eyes."
