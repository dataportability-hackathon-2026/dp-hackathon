# New York Times Co. v. Sullivan

**Citation:** 376 U.S. 254 (1964)
**Year:** 1964
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Constitutional Law

## Facts
L.B. Sullivan, a city commissioner in Montgomery, Alabama, sued The New York Times for libel over a full-page advertisement that described civil rights abuses by police. The ad contained several minor factual inaccuracies. An Alabama jury awarded Sullivan $500,000 in damages, and the Alabama Supreme Court affirmed, applying the state's standard that did not require proof of fault for defamation of public officials.

## Issue
Whether the First and Fourteenth Amendments limit a state's power to award damages in a libel action brought by a public official against critics of his official conduct.

## Holding
The Court held unanimously that the First Amendment requires a public official to prove "actual malice" to recover damages for defamation. Actual malice means the statement was made with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not. The Court found that the evidence did not support a finding of actual malice by The New York Times.

## Significance
This decision fundamentally reshaped American defamation law by establishing the "actual malice" standard for public officials. It provided robust protections for press freedom and public debate, recognizing that a free society requires uninhibited, robust, and wide-open discussion of public issues. The standard was later extended to public figures in Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts.

## Key Quotes
> "Debate on public issues should be uninhibited, robust, and wide-open, and it may well include vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks on government and public officials."
