# Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.

**Citation:** 464 U.S. 417 (1984)
**Year:** 1984
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Intellectual Property / Technology

## Facts
Universal City Studios and Walt Disney Productions sued Sony, the manufacturer of the Betamax videocassette recorder, alleging that Sony was liable for contributory copyright infringement because consumers used the Betamax to record copyrighted television programs. The plaintiffs argued that home taping constituted copyright infringement and that Sony, by selling the technology that enabled it, was legally responsible.

## Issue
Whether the sale of home videotape recorders to the general public constitutes contributory infringement of copyrights held by television program owners.

## Holding
The Court held 5-4 that Sony was not liable for contributory infringement. The Court found that the Betamax was capable of commercially significant noninfringing uses, primarily "time-shifting" — recording programs to watch at a later time. Time-shifting for private, noncommercial use constituted fair use. Because the device was capable of substantial noninfringing uses, Sony could not be held liable for selling it.

## Significance
The Sony decision, often called the "Betamax case," established the staple article of commerce doctrine for technology products. It held that manufacturers of devices capable of substantial noninfringing uses cannot be held liable for contributory copyright infringement. The ruling has been foundational in technology law, protecting manufacturers of dual-use technologies from VCRs to computers to file-sharing software.

## Key Quotes
> "The sale of copying equipment, like the sale of other articles of commerce, does not constitute contributory infringement if the product is widely used for legitimate, unobjectionable purposes."
