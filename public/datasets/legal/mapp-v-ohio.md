# Mapp v. Ohio

**Citation:** 367 U.S. 643 (1961)
**Year:** 1961
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Criminal Procedure

## Facts
Cleveland police officers arrived at Dollree Mapp's home seeking a suspect in a bombing case and demanded entry. When Mapp refused without a warrant, officers forcibly entered. They produced a piece of paper they claimed was a warrant, which Mapp grabbed and placed in her clothing. Officers retrieved it, handcuffed her, and conducted a widespread search of the entire house. They found obscene materials and charged Mapp with possession of obscene materials, though no bombing suspect was found.

## Issue
Whether the exclusionary rule, which prohibits the use of illegally obtained evidence in federal courts, applies to state courts through the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Holding
The Court held 6-3 that the exclusionary rule applies to state courts through the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Evidence obtained through searches and seizures that violate the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible in state court proceedings. The Court overruled Wolf v. Colorado (1949), which had recognized the Fourth Amendment's applicability to states but declined to require the exclusionary remedy.

## Significance
Mapp v. Ohio extended the exclusionary rule to all courts in the United States, creating a uniform standard for the admissibility of evidence. The decision fundamentally changed police practices by creating a strong incentive for law enforcement to comply with Fourth Amendment requirements. It remains one of the most important decisions in criminal procedure, though the scope of the exclusionary rule has been narrowed by subsequent decisions.

## Key Quotes
> "The criminal goes free, if he must, but it is the law that sets him free. Nothing can destroy a government more quickly than its failure to observe its own laws."
