# Weeks v. United States

**Citation:** 232 U.S. 383 (1914)
**Year:** 1914
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Criminal Procedure

## Facts
Fremont Weeks was arrested at his workplace by a police officer without a warrant. Other officers and a U.S. Marshal went to his home, also without a warrant, and searched it. They seized papers, documents, and other personal effects. Additional warrantless searches followed. The seized materials were used as evidence to convict Weeks of using the mails for illegal lottery operations. Weeks petitioned for the return of his property before trial, but the court denied the request.

## Issue
Whether evidence obtained through a warrantless search and seizure by federal officers may be used against the defendant in a federal criminal proceeding, and whether the Fourth Amendment requires the exclusion of such evidence.

## Holding
The Court held unanimously that the Fourth Amendment bars the use of evidence obtained through unreasonable searches and seizures in federal prosecutions. The Court reasoned that if illegally obtained evidence could be used at trial, the Fourth Amendment would be of no value and might as well be stricken from the Constitution. The letters and papers seized must be returned to Weeks.

## Significance
Weeks established the exclusionary rule for federal courts, creating the principle that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment cannot be used in criminal proceedings. This was the first time the Supreme Court enforced the Fourth Amendment by excluding evidence. The rule was later extended to state courts in Mapp v. Ohio (1961), making it a cornerstone of American criminal procedure.

## Key Quotes
> "If letters and private documents can thus be seized and held and used in evidence against a citizen accused of an offense, the protection of the Fourth Amendment declaring his right to be secure against such searches and seizures is of no value, and might as well be stricken from the Constitution."
