# Escola v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.

**Citation:** 24 Cal.2d 453, 150 P.2d 436 (1944)
**Year:** 1944
**Court:** Supreme Court of California
**Topic Area:** Torts

## Facts
Gladys Escola, a waitress, was placing bottles of Coca-Cola into a refrigerator when one of the bottles exploded in her hand, causing severe injuries. She sued the Coca-Cola Bottling Company of Fresno. Escola could not prove exactly what caused the bottle to explode but presented evidence that the bottle had not been subjected to unusual treatment after leaving the bottler's possession. The company inspected its bottles but acknowledged that defective bottles could occasionally pass inspection.

## Issue
Whether a manufacturer should be held liable for injuries caused by a defective product under the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur, and whether strict liability should apply to manufacturers.

## Holding
The majority applied the doctrine of res ipsa loquitur, finding that bottles of carbonated beverages do not ordinarily explode and that the bottler had exclusive control over the bottling process. This created an inference of negligence sufficient to support the verdict. Justice Traynor's influential concurrence argued that the court should go further and adopt strict liability for manufacturers, reasoning that public policy demands the manufacturer bear responsibility for injuries caused by defective products.

## Significance
While the majority opinion applied traditional negligence principles, Justice Traynor's concurrence is considered one of the most influential opinions in tort law history. His argument for strict product liability was eventually adopted by the California Supreme Court in Greenman v. Yuba Power Products (1963) and subsequently by courts across the country. The case is a landmark in the development of modern strict liability doctrine.

## Key Quotes
> "Even if there is no negligence, however, public policy demands that responsibility be fixed wherever it will most effectively reduce the hazards to life and health inherent in defective products that reach the market."
