# Smith v. Maryland

**Citation:** 442 U.S. 735 (1979)
**Year:** 1979
**Court:** Supreme Court of the United States
**Topic Area:** Data Privacy

## Facts
After a robbery, the victim began receiving threatening phone calls from someone claiming to be the robber. Police identified Michael Lee Smith as a suspect and, without a warrant, asked the telephone company to install a pen register on his phone line. The pen register recorded the numbers dialed from Smith's phone, revealing that he had called the victim. Smith was arrested and moved to suppress the pen register evidence.

## Issue
Whether the installation and use of a pen register to record the numbers dialed from a telephone constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment.

## Holding
The Court held 5-3 that the use of a pen register is not a search because Smith had no reasonable expectation of privacy in the phone numbers he dialed. By voluntarily conveying the numbers to the telephone company in the ordinary course of business, Smith assumed the risk that the company would reveal that information to the police. The Fourth Amendment does not prohibit obtaining information revealed to a third party.

## Significance
Smith v. Maryland established the third-party doctrine, which holds that a person has no legitimate expectation of privacy in information voluntarily turned over to third parties. This doctrine became enormously influential in the digital age, though the Supreme Court later limited its scope in Carpenter v. United States (2018) with respect to cell-site location information.

## Key Quotes
> "This Court consistently has held that a person has no legitimate expectation of privacy in information he voluntarily turns over to third parties."
